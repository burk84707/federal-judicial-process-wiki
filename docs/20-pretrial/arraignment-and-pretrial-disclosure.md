# Arraignment and Pretrial Disclosure

## Verification boundary

This module separates five sources that are often collapsed into “discovery”: Rule 16 party discovery, the constitutional *Brady* line, the Jencks Act, Rule 26.2 witness-statement production, and Rule 17 subpoenas. It supplies their nationwide text and Supreme Court boundaries. It does not set a universal *Brady* production date, decide whether a particular item is favorable or material, define the prosecution team for a specific investigation, resolve privilege or classified-information procedure, apply local discovery orders, or decide circuit-specific remedies and standards of review.

## Arraignment under Rule 10

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-001 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
Rule 10 requires an arraignment in open court at which the court ensures that the defendant has the indictment or information, reads it or states the substance of the charge, and asks the defendant to plead. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 10(a)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-002 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2,AUTH-FRCRIM-P-23-24-26-30-31-43 status:verified_current -->
Under Rule 10(b), a defendant charged by indictment or misdemeanor information need not be present at arraignment if the defendant, in a written waiver signed by both the defendant and defense counsel, has waived appearance and affirmed receipt of the charging document and that the plea is not guilty, and the court accepts the waiver. Rule 43(b) separately excuses presence for an organization represented by counsel who is present and, for an offense punishable by fine or imprisonment of not more than one year, or both, when the court, with the defendant's written consent, permits arraignment by video teleconferencing or in the defendant's absence. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 10(b)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf); [Fed. R. Crim. P. 43(a)–(b)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-003 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
Rule 10(c) permits arraignment by video teleconference only with the defendant's consent. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 10(c)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

## Rule 16 party discovery

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-004 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
Rule 16(a)(1) creates specified government-disclosure duties upon a defendant's request; it is not a general command to open every government file. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(a)(1)–(2)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-005 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
The statement provisions distinguish a relevant oral statement made in known-agent interrogation that the government intends to use at trial from the broader specified written, recorded, recorded-oral, and grand-jury statements covered by Rule 16(a)(1)(B). [Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(a)(1)(A)–(B)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-006 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
On request and subject to the rule's possession, knowledge, materiality, intended-use, and ownership conditions, Rule 16(a)(1) also covers the defendant's prior record, documents and objects, examination and test reports, and expert-witness information. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(a)(1)(D)–(G)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-007 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
For Rule 16 expert disclosures, the court by order or local rule must set a time sufficiently before trial to give the opposing side a fair opportunity to meet the evidence, and the current rule specifies the required opinions, bases, reasons, qualifications, prior publications, prior testimony, approval, signature, and supplementation information. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(a)(1)(G), (b)(1)(C)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-008 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2,AUTH-USC-18-3500 status:verified_current -->
Except as permitted by Rule 16(a)(1)(A)–(D), (F), and (G), Rule 16(a)(2) does not authorize the discovery or inspection of reports, memoranda, or other internal government documents made by an attorney for the government or other government agent in connection with investigating or prosecuting the case. Rule 16(a)(2) also does not authorize the discovery or inspection of statements made by prospective government witnesses except as provided in 18 U.S.C. § 3500. Under Rule 16(a)(3), Rule 16 does not apply to the discovery or inspection of a grand jury's recorded proceedings, except as provided in Rules 6, 12(h), 16(a)(1), and 26.2. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(a)(2)–(3)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf); [18 U.S.C. § 3500](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3500)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-009 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
The defendant's reciprocal duties arise only under the conditions stated in Rule 16(b): the document duty applies, upon government request, if the defendant requested disclosure under Rule 16(a)(1)(E) and the government complied, and the report duty applies, upon government request, if the defendant requested disclosure under Rule 16(a)(1)(F) and the government complied, and in either case only for items within the defendant's possession, custody, or control that the defendant intends to use in the defendant's case-in-chief at trial or, for a report, that relate to the testimony of the witness who prepared it, if the defendant intends to call that witness; the expert-disclosure duty applies at the government's request, for expert testimony the defendant intends to use during the defendant's case-in-chief at trial, if the defendant requested disclosure under Rule 16(a)(1)(G) and the government complied, or if the defendant has given notice under Rule 12.2(b) of an intent to present expert testimony on the defendant's mental condition. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(b)(1)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-010 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
Rule 16(c) imposes a continuing duty to disclose newly discovered evidence or material before or during trial when it is subject to Rule 16 and the opposing party previously requested, or the court ordered, production. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(c)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-011 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
For good cause, Rule 16(d)(1) permits a court to deny, restrict, or defer discovery, or grant other appropriate relief, including on an ex parte written showing whose complete text the court must preserve under seal if relief is granted. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(d)(1)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-012 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
For a Rule 16 violation, the court may order discovery on just terms, grant a continuance, prohibit use of undisclosed evidence, or enter another order just under the circumstances; the rule does not make one sanction automatic for every violation. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(d)(2)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-013 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
No later than 14 days after arraignment, Rule 16.1(a) requires government and defense counsel to confer and try to agree on a timetable and procedures for Rule 16 disclosure; after that conference, either or both may ask the court to determine or modify disclosure arrangements. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 16.1](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-014 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-45 status:verified_current -->
Rule 45(a) supplies federal criminal time computation for periods in the rules, local rules, court orders, and statutes that do not specify a method; under Rule 45(b)(1), the court on its own may extend the time, or for good cause may do so on a party's motion made before the time expires or, after it expires, if the party failed to act because of excusable neglect. Rule 16.1 is not within Rule 45(b)(2)'s exception, which covers only action under Rule 35. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 45(a)–(b)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title18/pdf/USCODE-2024-title18-app.pdf)

## Constitutional disclosure is a separate analysis

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-015 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2,AUTH-USC-18-3500,AUTH-SCOTUS-BRADY-373-US-83,AUTH-SCOTUS-GIGLIO-405-US-150 status:verified_current -->
Rule 16, the Jencks Act, and the *Brady* line are separate sources with different triggers and coverage, so an item may require independent classification under more than one source rather than treatment under a single generic “discovery” rule. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 16](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf); [18 U.S.C. § 3500](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3500); [*Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 86–88 (1963)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-373/pdf/USREPORTS-373-83.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-016 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-BRADY-373-US-83,AUTH-SCOTUS-STRICKLER-527-US-263 status:verified_current -->
A true *Brady* violation has three components: evidence favorable to the accused, suppression by the government willfully or inadvertently, and resulting prejudice. [*Strickler v. Greene*, 527 U.S. 263, 281–82 (1999)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-527/pdf/USREPORTS-527-263.pdf); [*Brady*, 373 U.S. at 87](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-373/pdf/USREPORTS-373-83.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-017 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-GIGLIO-405-US-150,AUTH-SCOTUS-BAGLEY-473-US-667,AUTH-SCOTUS-STRICKLER-527-US-263 status:verified_current -->
Evidence may be favorable because it is exculpatory or because it impeaches a prosecution witness; impeachment evidence is not categorically outside the constitutional disclosure rule. [*Strickler*, 527 U.S. at 281–82](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-527/pdf/USREPORTS-527-263.pdf); [*United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667, 676–78 (1985)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-473/pdf/USREPORTS-473-667.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-018 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-BAGLEY-473-US-667 status:verified_current -->
The constitutional disclosure duty is not eliminated by the absence of a specific defense request: in Part III of *Bagley*, Justice Blackmun, joined only by Justice O'Connor, found the materiality standard sufficiently flexible to cover no-request, general-request, and specific-request nondisclosure, and Justice White's concurrence for three Justices agreed it covers all instances of prosecutorial failure to disclose favorable evidence. [*United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667, 682–83 (1985) (opinion of Blackmun, J.)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-473/pdf/USREPORTS-473-667.pdf); [*id.* at 685 (White, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-473/pdf/USREPORTS-473-667.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-019 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-BRADY-373-US-83,AUTH-SCOTUS-STRICKLER-527-US-263 status:verified_current -->
The prosecution's good faith does not defeat a *Brady* claim, because suppression may be willful or inadvertent; prejudice and the other constitutional components remain required. [*Brady*, 373 U.S. at 87](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-373/pdf/USREPORTS-373-83.pdf); [*Strickler*, 527 U.S. at 281–82](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-527/pdf/USREPORTS-527-263.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-020 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-BAGLEY-473-US-667 status:verified_current -->
Under *Bagley*, suppressed favorable evidence is material only if there is a reasonable probability that, had it been disclosed to the defense, the result of the proceeding would have been different; a reasonable probability is a probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the outcome. This formulation appears in Justice Blackmun's Part III opinion, joined only by Justice O'Connor, and was adopted by Justice White's three-Justice concurrence. [*United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667, 682 (1985) (opinion of Blackmun, J.)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-473/pdf/USREPORTS-473-667.pdf); [*id.* at 685 (White, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-473/pdf/USREPORTS-473-667.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-021 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-KYLES-514-US-419 status:verified_current -->
*Kyles* requires materiality to be assessed collectively, not item by item, and makes clear that the inquiry is not a sufficiency-of-the-remaining-evidence test or a constitutional open-file mandate. [*Kyles v. Whitley*, 514 U.S. 419, 434–37 (1995)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-514/pdf/USREPORTS-514-419.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-022 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-KYLES-514-US-419 status:verified_current -->
The individual prosecutor must learn of favorable evidence known to others acting on the government's behalf in the case, including police, but determining who falls within that case-specific group requires the actual investigative relationships and controlling law. [*Kyles*, 514 U.S. at 437–38](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-514/pdf/USREPORTS-514-419.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-023 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-GIGLIO-405-US-150 status:verified_current -->
*Giglio* treats an undisclosed promise of nonprosecution to a key government witness as credibility evidence within the due-process rule when the witness's reliability may determine guilt or innocence; it does not require a new trial for every item that might have been useful to the defense. [*Giglio v. United States*, 405 U.S. 150, 153–55 (1972)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-405/pdf/USREPORTS-405-150.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-024 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-GIGLIO-405-US-150 status:verified_current -->
For the promise at issue in *Giglio*, the prosecutor's office was treated as the government entity, so a promise by one assistant was attributable to the government despite the trial prosecutor's lack of personal knowledge. [*Giglio*, 405 U.S. at 154](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-405/pdf/USREPORTS-405-150.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-025 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-NAPUE-360-US-264,AUTH-SCOTUS-GIGLIO-405-US-150 status:verified_current -->
The distinct *Napue* rule forbids the government from knowingly using false testimony or allowing known false testimony to go uncorrected, including falsehoods bearing on witness credibility; the materiality question asks whether there is any reasonable likelihood that the false testimony could have affected the jury's judgment. [*Napue v. Illinois*, 360 U.S. 264, 269–72 (1959)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-360/pdf/USREPORTS-360-264.pdf); [*Giglio*, 405 U.S. at 153–54](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-405/pdf/USREPORTS-405-150.pdf)

The Supreme Court authorities above do not supply one calendar date for every constitutional disclosure. The governing discovery order, local rule, trial schedule, proposed use of the evidence, and controlling circuit timing law must be checked without confusing *Bagley*'s post hoc materiality standard with a license to delay disclosure.

## Jencks Act and Rule 26.2 witness statements

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-026 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3500 status:verified_current -->
In a federal criminal prosecution, § 3500(a) bars subpoena, discovery, or inspection of any statement or report in United States possession made by a government witness or prospective government witness (other than the defendant) until that witness has testified on direct examination in the trial of the case. [18 U.S.C. § 3500(a)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3500)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-027 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3500 status:verified_current -->
After a witness called by the United States testifies on direct, § 3500(b) requires the court, on the defendant's motion, to order the United States to produce any statement of the witness in its possession that relates to the subject matter of the testimony. When a statement is delivered, § 3500(c) provides that the court in its discretion, upon the defendant's application, may recess proceedings for the time reasonably required to examine the statement and prepare for its use. [18 U.S.C. § 3500(b)–(c)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3500)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-028 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3500 status:verified_current -->
If the United States claims that a statement contains matter unrelated to the witness's testimony, § 3500(c) requires in camera inspection, excision of unrelated portions, and delivery of the remainder to the defendant. If any portion is withheld, the defendant objects, and the trial is continued to an adjudication of guilt, the entire text of the statement shall be preserved by the United States and, in the event the defendant appeals, made available to the appellate court. [18 U.S.C. § 3500(c)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3500)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-029 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3500 status:verified_current -->
If the United States elects not to obey a § 3500 production order, subsection (d) requires striking the witness's testimony and continuing trial unless the court determines that the interests of justice require a mistrial. [18 U.S.C. § 3500(d)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3500)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-030 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3500 status:verified_current -->
As used in § 3500(b), (c), and (d) in relation to a witness called by the United States, “statement” means a written statement made by the witness and signed or otherwise adopted or approved by the witness, a substantially verbatim contemporaneous recording or transcription of the witness's oral statement, or the witness's statement to a grand jury. [18 U.S.C. § 3500(e)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3500)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-031 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
Rule 26.2(a) is reciprocal: after a nondefendant witness testifies on direct, the court must, on motion by a party who did not call the witness, order the calling side to produce a possessed statement relating to the testimony's subject matter. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 26.2(a)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-032 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
Rule 26.2 applies at trial, at a suppression hearing under Rule 12, and to the extent specified in Rules 5.1(h), 32(i)(2), 32.1(e), and 46(j) and Rule 8 of the Rules Governing Proceedings under 28 U.S.C. § 2255; if the party who called the witness disobeys an order to produce or deliver a statement, the court must strike the witness's testimony, and if an attorney for the government disobeys, the court must declare a mistrial if justice so requires. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 26.2(e), (g)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

## Rule 17 subpoenas

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-033 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
Rule 17(a) prescribes the contents of a witness subpoena, and Rule 17(b) requires the court, on a defendant's ex parte application, to order a subpoena issued for a named witness if the defendant shows an inability to pay the witness's fees and the necessity of the witness's presence for an adequate defense, with process costs and witness fees paid as for government witnesses. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 17(a)–(b)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-034 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
Current Rule 17(c) permits a subpoena to designate documents or objects for production, permits the court, on motion made promptly, to quash or modify the subpoena if compliance would be unreasonable or oppressive, and requires a court order and, unless there are exceptional circumstances, notice to the victim before a post-charge subpoena for a victim's personal or confidential information is served on a third party. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 17(c)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-035 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-10-16-17-26-2 status:verified_current -->
Current Rule 17(h) prohibits using Rule 17 to subpoena a witness or prospective witness's statement and directs the parties to Rule 26.2 instead. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 17(h)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-036 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-NIXON-418-US-683 status:verified_current -->
Construing the 1974 text of Rule 17(c), *United States v. Nixon* recognized that the subpoena duces tecum was not intended to provide a means of discovery for criminal cases, described the showing lower courts had generally followed (evidentiary and relevant material not otherwise procurable reasonably in advance of trial, need for advance inspection, and good faith rather than a general fishing expedition), and held that the Special Prosecutor must clear three hurdles: relevancy, admissibility, and specificity. *Nixon* expressly did not decide whether a lower standard exists when the subpoena is issued to third parties rather than to government prosecutors. [*United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 698–702 & n.12 (1974)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-418/pdf/USREPORTS-418-683.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-DISC-037 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-PROP-17-2026,AUTH-RULES-PACKAGE-2026-10-20 status:verified_current -->
Proposed amendments to Rule 17 are pending; they remain proposals, not current Rule 17. After a public-comment period from August 2025 to February 2026, the Standing Committee in June 2026 approved final proposed amendments for submission to the Judicial Conference. The Judicial Conference has approved the amendments, and on October 20, 2026, by direction of the Judicial Conference, they were transmitted to the Supreme Court with a recommendation that the Court adopt them and transmit them to Congress. According to the transmittal package, if the amendments are adopted by the Court and transmitted to Congress by May 1, 2027, absent congressional action, they will take effect on December 1, 2027. [Proposed Fed. R. Crim. P. 17 amendment (Standing Committee approval, June 2026)](https://www.uscourts.gov/forms-rules/records-rules-committees/suggestions/new-york-city-bar-association-22-cr-a); [October 20, 2026 Supreme Court package](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/2026_scotus_package.pdf)

## Proof and record checklist

Before applying any branch, collect the charging document and arraignment record; every discovery request and response; the district's local criminal rules and discovery order; the government's exhibit and witness notices; the allegedly suppressed item and evidence of who possessed it when; the relationship among prosecutors, agents, police, and other investigators; witness agreements, benefits, promises, payments, and correction history; the proposed use and admissibility of subpoenaed material; the exact witness statement and direct testimony; all production, protective, continuance, exclusion, mistrial, and preservation orders; and the governing circuit's timing, remedy, and standard-of-review cases.

## Common failure points

- Treating Rule 16, *Brady*, § 3500, Rule 26.2, and Rule 17 as interchangeable.
- Calling every favorable nondisclosure a completed constitutional violation without proving suppression and prejudice.
- Applying *Bagley* item by item rather than cumulatively.
- Assuming the trial prosecutor's lack of personal knowledge ends the prosecution-team inquiry.
- Treating impeachment nondisclosure and known false testimony as the same materiality branch.
- Demanding Jencks Act statements before the statutory trigger without separately analyzing constitutional disclosure.
- Using Rule 17(c) as an unspecific document-discovery request.
- Applying the proposed 2027 Rule 17 text as if already effective.
- Missing the Rule 16.1 conference date, local deadlines, preservation objections, or a remedy-specific motion.

## Open questions requiring narrower authority

The following remain outside this nationwide module: circuit-specific constitutional disclosure timing; circuit definitions of the prosecution team; treatment of evidence known to state, foreign, civil, intelligence, or separate investigative bodies; consequences of delayed but pre-use disclosure; preservation and waiver; classified-information and privilege procedure; grand-jury disclosure; electronic-data production protocols; local expert schedules; ex parte subpoena practice beyond current Rule 17(b); interlocutory review; and appellate standards for each sanction or constitutional disposition.
