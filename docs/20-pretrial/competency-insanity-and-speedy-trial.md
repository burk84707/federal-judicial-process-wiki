# Competency, Insanity Procedure, and Speedy Trial

## Verification boundary

Competency concerns present ability to proceed; insanity concerns responsibility at the time of the charged act. The Speedy Trial Act supplies statutory clocks and exclusions; the Sixth Amendment supplies a separate postaccusation balancing test. This module does not diagnose a person, calculate a live docket without every entry, resolve dangerousness, authorize involuntary medication in a particular case, apply a district plan, or decide circuit-specific appellate and collateral-review questions.

## Present competency

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-001 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-DUSKY-362-US-402 status:verified_current -->
The competency test adopted in *Dusky* asks whether the defendant has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding and has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings; it is not enough that the defendant is oriented to time and place and has some recollection of events. [*Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-362/pdf/USREPORTS-362-402.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-002 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-17-4241-4243-4247 status:verified_current -->
From commencement of prosecution until sentencing, or from commencement of probation or supervised release until completion of the sentence, either side may move for a competency hearing, and the court shall grant it or order one on its own motion when there is reasonable cause to believe a present mental disease or defect may prevent understanding the proceedings or assisting properly in the defense. [18 U.S.C. § 4241(a)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4241)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-003 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-17-4241-4243-4247 status:verified_current -->
Before the hearing, the court may order a psychiatric or psychological examination and report under § 4247(b)–(c); at the hearing, § 4247(d) provides counsel plus opportunities to testify, present evidence, subpoena witnesses, and confront and cross-examine appearing witnesses. [18 U.S.C. §§ 4241(b)–(c), 4247(d)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4247)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-004 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-17-4241-4243-4247 status:verified_current -->
If the court finds present incompetency by a preponderance of the evidence, § 4241(d) requires commitment to the Attorney General for treatment in a suitable facility for a reasonable period not exceeding four months to determine whether there is a substantial probability of foreseeable restoration. [18 U.S.C. § 4241(d)(1)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4241)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-005 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-17-4241-4243-4247 status:verified_current -->
An additional period of hospitalization must be reasonable and requires a court finding of a substantial probability that the defendant will attain capacity within that period; it ends when his condition is so improved that trial may proceed or the pending charges are disposed of according to law, whichever is earlier, and a defendant not so improved at the end of the period is subject to §§ 4246 and 4248. [18 U.S.C. § 4241(d)(2)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4241)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-006 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-17-4241-4243-4247 status:verified_current -->
For an examination under § 4241, § 4247(b) permits examination commitment for a reasonable period not exceeding 30 days and a facility-requested extension not exceeding 15 days on the required good-cause showing. [18 U.S.C. § 4247(b)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4247)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-007 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-17-4241-4243-4247 status:verified_current -->
When a facility director certifies recovery, the court must hold a § 4247(d) hearing and order immediate discharge from hospitalization and set trial if it finds restored competency by a preponderance. [18 U.S.C. § 4241(e)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4241)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-008 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-17-4241-4243-4247 status:verified_current -->
A competency finding neither prejudices a later insanity defense nor is admissible at the trial on the charged offense. [18 U.S.C. § 4241(f)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4241)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-009 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-PATE-383-US-375 status:verified_current -->
When evidence raises the constitutionally sufficient doubt about present competence, failure to conduct the required inquiry deprives the defendant of a fair trial; the issue cannot be treated as waived merely because defense counsel did not obtain the hearing. [*Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 384–86 (1966)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-383/pdf/USREPORTS-383-375.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-010 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-DROPE-420-US-162 status:verified_current -->
The duty to protect against trial of an incompetent defendant is continuing: the court must consider the full record, including prior history, behavior, counsel's representations, and developments during trial, rather than rely on one examination or courtroom observation alone. [*Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 171–81 (1975)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-420/pdf/USREPORTS-420-162.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-011 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-SELL-539-US-166 status:verified_current -->
*Sell* permits involuntary medication of a defendant facing serious criminal charges solely to restore trial competency in instances that may be rare, and only if the court finds important governmental interests at stake, judged on the facts of the individual case because special circumstances may lessen that interest; a substantial likelihood of restoring competency and a substantial unlikelihood of side effects that will interfere significantly with the defendant's ability to assist counsel; necessity, meaning that any alternative, less intrusive treatments are unlikely to achieve substantially the same results, with less intrusive means of administering the drugs considered before more intrusive methods; and medical appropriateness. [*Sell v. United States*, 539 U.S. 166, 179–81 (2003)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-539/pdf/USREPORTS-539-166.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-012 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-SELL-539-US-166 status:verified_current -->
Before reaching a trial-competency medication order, a court ordinarily should consider whether forced medication is warranted on a different ground such as dangerousness, and *Sell* treats a qualifying medication order as immediately appealable under the collateral-order doctrine. [*Sell*, 539 U.S. at 175–77, 182–83](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-539/pdf/USREPORTS-539-166.pdf)

## Insanity defense and postverdict procedure

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-013 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-17-4241-4243-4247 status:verified_current -->
Federal insanity is an affirmative defense requiring proof that, because of a severe mental disease or defect at the time of the acts, the defendant could not appreciate their nature and quality or wrongfulness; the defendant bears clear-and-convincing proof, and mental disease or defect does not otherwise constitute the statutory defense. [18 U.S.C. § 17](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/17)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-014 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-12-14-41-47-51-52 status:verified_current -->
A defendant intending to assert insanity must give written government notice and file it by the pretrial-motion deadline or a later court-set time; absent notice the defense is unavailable, subject to the court's good-cause power to permit late notice and other appropriate relief. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.2(a)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-015 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-12-14-41-47-51-52 status:verified_current -->
Rule 12.2(b) separately requires pretrial notice of expert mental-condition evidence bearing on guilt or capital punishment; giving or omitting that notice is not the same as asserting the statutory insanity defense. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.2(b)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-016 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-12-14-41-47-51-52,AUTH-USC-18-4242 status:verified_current -->
After Rule 12.2(a) insanity notice, the court must, upon the government's motion, order an examination under § 4242; after Rule 12.2(b) notice, it may, upon the government's motion, order an examination under procedures ordered by the court. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.2(c)(1)(B)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf); [18 U.S.C. § 4242(a)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4242?link-type=html)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-017 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-12-14-41-47-51-52 status:verified_current -->
No statement made during a Rule 12.2 examination, no expert testimony based on it, and no other fruits of it may be admitted against the defendant in any criminal proceeding, except on a mental-condition issue on which the defendant has introduced evidence of incompetency or evidence requiring Rule 12.2(a) or (b)(1) notice, or has introduced expert evidence in a capital sentencing proceeding requiring Rule 12.2(b)(2) notice. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.2(c)(4)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-018 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-12-14-41-47-51-52 status:verified_current -->
Under Rule 12.2(d)(1), the court may exclude any expert evidence from the defendant on mental condition bearing on guilt or on punishment in a capital case if the defendant fails to give Rule 12.2(b) notice or to submit to an examination ordered under Rule 12.2(c); under Rule 12.2(d)(2), it may exclude any expert evidence for which the defendant has failed to comply with Rule 12.2(c)(3)'s disclosure requirement. Separately, under Rule 12.2(a) a defendant who fails to give the required insanity notice cannot rely on an insanity defense, although the court may, for good cause, allow late notice. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.2(a), (d)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-019 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-12-14-41-47-51-52 status:verified_current -->
Evidence of a Rule 12.2(a) or (b) intention later withdrawn is inadmissible against the person who gave the notice in a civil or criminal proceeding. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.2(e)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-020 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-4242 status:verified_current -->
When the issue of insanity is raised by Rule 12.2 notice on motion of the defendant or the government, or on the court's own motion, § 4242(b) provides that the jury shall be instructed to find, or the court in a nonjury trial shall find, the defendant guilty, not guilty, or not guilty only by reason of insanity. [18 U.S.C. § 4242(b)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4242?link-type=html)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-021 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-4243 status:verified_current -->
After a federal verdict of not guilty only by reason of insanity, § 4243 requires commitment to a suitable facility until the person is eligible for release under subsection (e), a court-ordered psychiatric or psychological examination before the hearing, and a hearing no later than forty days after the special verdict to determine whether release would create the statutory substantial risk. [18 U.S.C. § 4243(a)–(d)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4243?link-type=html)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-022 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-17-4241-4243-4247 status:verified_current -->
At the § 4243 hearing, the acquitted person bears clear-and-convincing proof for an offense involving bodily injury, serious property damage, or a substantial risk of either, and a preponderance burden for any other offense, to show release would not create the specified risk due to present mental disease or defect. [18 U.S.C. § 4243(d)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4243)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-023 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-4243,AUTH-USC-18-4247 status:verified_current -->
If the acquitted person does not carry the § 4243 burden, the statute requires Attorney General custody, reasonable efforts to have a State assume responsibility, and hospitalization until a State assumes responsibility or release or conditional release would not create the statutory risk, whichever is earlier; later discharge and conditional-release procedure is governed by § 4243(f)–(g). Separately, counsel or a legal guardian may move for a discharge hearing at any time during commitment, but not within 180 days of a court determination that commitment should continue, and habeas corpus remains available. [18 U.S.C. § 4243(e)–(g)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4243?link-type=html); [18 U.S.C. § 4247(g)–(h)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/4247?link-type=html)

<!-- claim:CLM-MENTAL-024 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-SHANNON-512-US-573 status:verified_current -->
Federal juries ordinarily are not instructed about the commitment consequences of a not-guilty-by-reason-of-insanity verdict because juries determine guilt rather than punishment or disposition; *Shannon* recognizes a limited corrective-instruction situation when a false impression about immediate release has been created. [*Shannon v. United States*, 512 U.S. 573, 579–88 (1994)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-512/pdf/USREPORTS-512-573.pdf)

## Speedy Trial Act computation and remedy

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-001 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3161-3162 status:verified_current -->
The 30-day charging clock runs from arrest or service of a summons and is extended an additional thirty days for a felony charged in a district where no grand jury has been in session during that period; where a plea of not guilty is entered, the 70-day trial clock runs from the filing date (and making public) of the information or indictment or from the defendant's appearance before a judicial officer of the court in which the charge is pending, whichever date last occurs. Both clocks are computed after excluding the periods of delay listed in § 3161(h). [18 U.S.C. § 3161(b), (c)(1), (h)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3161)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-002 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3161-3162,AUTH-SCOTUS-TINKLENBERG-563-US-647 status:verified_current -->
Delay from filing a pretrial motion through the hearing or prompt disposition is automatically excluded under § 3161(h)(1)(D); *Tinklenberg* holds that the motion need not actually postpone trial to trigger that exclusion. [*United States v. Tinklenberg*, 563 U.S. 647, 650, 655–60 (2011)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-563/pdf/USREPORTS-563-647.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-003 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-BLOATE-559-US-196 status:verified_current -->
*Bloate* holds that time granted to prepare pretrial motions is not automatically excludable under § 3161(h)(1); the Court explained that a district court may exclude such time under (h)(7) if it grants a continuance for that purpose based on recorded ends-of-justice findings, and it did not consider whether any other exclusion would apply to all or part of the period at issue. [*Bloate v. United States*, 559 U.S. 196, 204, 214–15 (2010)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-559/pdf/USREPORTS-559-196.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-004 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-ZEDNER-547-US-489 status:verified_current -->
A defendant cannot prospectively waive the Speedy Trial Act “for all time,” because the Act protects the public interest as well as the defendant and requires delay to fit a statutory exclusion. [*Zedner v. United States*, 547 U.S. 489, 500–03 (2006)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-547/pdf/USREPORTS-547-489.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-005 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3161-3162,AUTH-SCOTUS-ZEDNER-547-US-489 status:verified_current -->
An ends-of-justice continuance excludes time only when the court weighs the statutory interests and states its reasons on the record; *Zedner* requires express findings at least by the ruling on a dismissal motion and rejects harmless-error substitution for missing findings. [18 U.S.C. § 3161(h)(7)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3161); [*Zedner*, 547 U.S. at 506–09](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-547/pdf/USREPORTS-547-489.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-006 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3161-3162 status:verified_current -->
Section 3161(h) excludes, among other periods, delay from other proceedings concerning the defendant (including proceedings, including examinations, to determine mental competency or physical capacity, trial on other charges, interlocutory appeals, pretrial motions, transfer or removal proceedings, transportation (with time in excess of ten days from the removal or transportation order to arrival presumed unreasonable), consideration of a proposed plea agreement, and up to thirty days under advisement), deferred prosecution under a written agreement approved by the court, absence or unavailability of the defendant or an essential witness, mental incompetence or physical inability to stand trial, when a charge is dismissed on the government's motion and a charge for the same offense, or an offense required to be joined with it, is later filed, the period from the dismissal to the date the time limit would have begun to run on the later charge had there been no previous charge, a reasonable period of delay when the defendant is joined for trial with a codefendant as to whom the time for trial has not run and no motion for severance has been granted, ends-of-justice continuances, and up to one year, ordered by the court on a party's application and a finding by a preponderance of the evidence that an official request has been made for evidence that reasonably appears, or reasonably appeared when the request was made, to be in a foreign country. [18 U.S.C. § 3161(h)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3161)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-007 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3161-3162,AUTH-SCOTUS-TAYLOR-487-US-326 status:verified_current -->
For a trial-clock violation, a timely pretrial or pre-plea defense motion requires dismissal, but dismissal may be with or without prejudice; *Taylor* holds there is no presumption for either result and requires reasoned consideration of the statutory factors and relevant prejudice. [18 U.S.C. § 3162(a)(2)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3162); [*United States v. Taylor*, 487 U.S. 326, 332–43 (1988)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-487/pdf/USREPORTS-487-326.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-008 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3162,AUTH-SCOTUS-ZEDNER-547-US-489 status:verified_current -->
Failure of the defendant to move for dismissal prior to trial or entry of a plea of guilty or nolo contendere waives the § 3162(a)(2) dismissal right; *Zedner* rejected the argument that this retrospective waiver implies that prospective waivers are allowed and held that a defendant may not prospectively waive the application of the Act. [18 U.S.C. § 3162(a)(2)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3162?link-type=html); [*Zedner v. United States*, 547 U.S. 489, 500, 502–03 (2006)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-547/pdf/USREPORTS-547-489.pdf)

## Sixth Amendment speedy-trial right

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-009 authorities:AUTH-FRCRIM-P-12-14-41-47-51-52 status:verified_current -->
A reasonably available constitutional speedy-trial objection that can be decided without a merits trial must be raised by pretrial motion under Rule 12(b)(3)(A)(iii), subject to the motion deadline and good-cause rule. [Fed. R. Crim. P. 12(b)(3)(A)(iii), (c)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-criminal-procedure.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-010 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-BARKER-407-US-514 status:verified_current -->
The Sixth Amendment inquiry balances length of delay, reason for delay, the defendant's assertion of the right, and prejudice; none is a necessary or sufficient condition by itself, and the length must first be presumptively prejudicial enough to trigger full analysis. [*Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514, 530–33 (1972)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-407/pdf/USREPORTS-407-514.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-011 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-DOGGETT-505-US-647 status:verified_current -->
*Doggett* holds that sufficiently excessive postindictment government negligence can support relief even without affirmative proof of particularized trial prejudice, but presumptive prejudice cannot alone carry the claim without regard to the other *Barker* criteria, its importance increases with the length of delay, and negligence unaccompanied by particularized trial prejudice must have lasted longer to warrant relief; where the government's negligence caused delay six times as long as that generally sufficient to trigger judicial review and the presumption of prejudice was neither extenuated, as by the defendant's acquiescence, nor persuasively rebutted, the defendant was entitled to relief. [*Doggett v. United States*, 505 U.S. 647, 655–58 (1992)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-505/pdf/USREPORTS-505-647.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-012 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-BETTERMAN-578-US-437 status:verified_current -->
The Sixth Amendment Speedy Trial Clause protects the accused from arrest or indictment through trial and does not apply once a defendant has been found guilty at trial or has pleaded guilty; *Betterman* reserved whether it applies to bifurcated proceedings in which sentencing-stage facts could increase the prescribed range and whether it reattaches upon renewed prosecution after a successful appeal, and, noting that due process serves as a backstop against exorbitant sentencing delay, expressed no opinion on how Betterman, who advanced no due-process claim, might fare under that standard. [*Betterman v. Montana*, 578 U.S. 437, 439, 441–42 & n.2, 448 (2016)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/578BV.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-013 authorities:AUTH-USC-18-3161-3162,AUTH-USC-18-3162,AUTH-SCOTUS-BARKER-407-US-514 status:verified_current -->
The Speedy Trial Act and the Sixth Amendment apply different tests: the Act sets day limits, subject to the § 3161(h) exclusions, and a trial-clock violation requires dismissal, with or without prejudice, on the defendant's motion, which is waived if not made before trial or plea; *Barker* found no constitutional basis for quantifying the right into a specified number of days, rejected the demand-waiver rule in favor of balancing length of delay, reason for delay, assertion of the right, and prejudice, and called dismissal the only possible remedy. [18 U.S.C. § 3161](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3161); [18 U.S.C. § 3162(a)(2)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/18/3162?link-type=html); [*Barker*, 407 U.S. at 522–23, 529–30](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-407/pdf/USREPORTS-407-514.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-SPEEDY-014 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-BARKER-407-US-514 status:verified_current -->
Because a completed Sixth Amendment speedy-trial violation cannot be cured by holding the delayed trial, dismissal of the charge is the required remedy; the severe consequence is one reason the *Barker* inquiry must be applied carefully to the full record. [*Barker*, 407 U.S. at 522](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-407/pdf/USREPORTS-407-514.pdf)

No live Speedy Trial Act or Sixth Amendment conclusion should be made without the arrest, complaint, indictment, appearance, plea, motion, competency, continuance, codefendant, interlocutory-appeal, trial, and dismissal-motion record.
