# § 2255 Cognizability, Default, Innocence, and Ineffective Assistance

## Verification boundary

This module supplies nationwide Supreme Court baselines. It does not decide whether a specific claim is constitutional, jurisdictional, defaulted, waived, previously decided, factually innocent, prejudicial, or supported by an adequate record. Relitigation rules, collateral waivers, nonconstitutional sentencing errors, and innocence of enhancements contain important circuit-specific branches.

## Cognizable error

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-023 authorities:AUTH-USC-28-2255,AUTH-SCOTUS-ADDONIZIO-442-US-178,AUTH-SCOTUS-HILL-368-US-424 status:verified_current -->
Section 2255 reaches sentences imposed in violation of the Constitution or laws of the United States, sentences imposed without jurisdiction, sentences in excess of the maximum authorized by law, and sentences otherwise subject to collateral attack. [28 U.S.C. § 2255(a)–(b)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/28/2255) The remedy does not encompass all claimed errors: outside jurisdictional and constitutional claims, an error of law does not provide a basis for collateral attack unless it constituted "a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice." [*United States v. Addonizio*, 442 U.S. 178, 185 (1979)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-442/pdf/USREPORTS-442-178.pdf); [*Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-368/pdf/USREPORTS-368-424.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-024 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-HILL-368-US-424 status:verified_current -->
A nonconstitutional violation of a criminal rule is not automatically cognizable: in *Hill*, the failure to invite the defendant to speak before sentence was neither jurisdictional nor constitutional, was not a fundamental defect inherently resulting in a complete miscarriage of justice or an omission inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure, and did not present exceptional circumstances where the need for the remedy is apparent. The Court decided only that § 2255 relief is unavailable when all that is shown is a failure to comply with the formal requirements of Rule 32(a), leaving open violations occurring with other aggravating circumstances. [*Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428–29 (1962)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-368/pdf/USREPORTS-368-424.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-025 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-DAVIS-417-US-333 status:verified_current -->
*Davis* allowed a federal statutory claim based on an intervening substantive-law change where the conviction and punishment were for conduct the law did not make criminal; the decision does not make every later statutory interpretation collaterally cognizable. [*Davis v. United States*, 417 U.S. 333, 342–47 (1974)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-417/pdf/USREPORTS-417-333.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-026 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-ADDONIZIO-442-US-178 status:verified_current -->
A sentencing judge's disappointed expectation about later Parole Commission administration of a lawful sentence was not a § 2255 defect in *Addonizio*: the judge has no enforceable expectations about actual release, and later Commission actions, whether or not they accord with the judge's expectations at sentencing, do not retroactively affect the validity of the final judgment. Absent a claim of constitutional error or lack of jurisdiction, or a sentence outside statutory limits, collateral relief requires an error of fact or law of the "fundamental" character that renders the entire proceeding irregular and invalid. [*United States v. Addonizio*, 442 U.S. 178, 185–90 (1979)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-442/pdf/USREPORTS-442-178.pdf)

## Procedural default and innocence gateways

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-027 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-FRADY-456-US-152 status:verified_current -->
Collateral review does not simply import direct-review plain error: for a trial error defaulted by failure to object and appeal, *Frady* required cause and actual prejudice. [*United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 164–70 (1982)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-456/pdf/USREPORTS-456-152.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-028 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-FRADY-456-US-152 status:verified_current -->
*Frady*'s actual-prejudice inquiry requires more than a possibility of prejudice and evaluates the error's effect in the complete proceeding; the exact cause rule and claim-specific prejudice standard still require controlling authority and record application. [*Frady*, 456 U.S. at 167–72](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-456/pdf/USREPORTS-456-152.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-029 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-BOUSLEY-523-US-614 status:verified_current -->
A defendant who failed to challenge the constitutional validity of a guilty plea on direct review procedurally defaulted that claim in *Bousley* and had to establish cause and actual prejudice or actual innocence. [*Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 621–24 (1998)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-523/pdf/USREPORTS-523-614.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-030 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-BOUSLEY-523-US-614 status:verified_current -->
The *Bousley* gateway requires factual innocence, not merely legal insufficiency; it opens review of an underlying constitutional claim rather than supplying a freestanding merits claim. [*Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 623–24](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-523/pdf/USREPORTS-523-614.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-031 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-BOUSLEY-523-US-614 status:verified_current -->
On a *Bousley* actual-innocence showing, the government is not limited to the existing record and may present any admissible evidence of guilt, even evidence not presented at the plea colloquy; separately, where the government has forgone more serious charges in the course of plea bargaining, the movant's innocence showing must also extend to those charges. *Bousley* did not extend the showing to a charge absent record evidence that the government elected not to bring it in exchange for the plea. [*Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 624](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-523/pdf/USREPORTS-523-614.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-032 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-SCHLUP-513-US-298 status:verified_current -->
The *Schlup* miscarriage-of-justice gateway requires new reliable evidence and a showing that, considering all the evidence, it is more likely than not that no reasonable juror would have found guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. [*Schlup v. Delo*, 513 U.S. 298, 324–32 (1995)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-513/pdf/USREPORTS-513-298.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-033 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-MCQUIGGIN-569-US-383,AUTH-SCOTUS-SCHLUP-513-US-298 status:verified_current -->
*McQuiggin* applied the *Schlup* standard through § 2244(d)'s time bar and held that unexplained delay bears on evidentiary reliability even though diligence is not a separate threshold; the holding arose on the state-habeas track. [*McQuiggin*, 569 U.S. at 386–401](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/569bv.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-034 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-MCQUIGGIN-569-US-383,AUTH-SCOTUS-BOUSLEY-523-US-614 status:verified_current -->
Neither *McQuiggin* nor *Bousley* establishes a universal gateway for innocence of a Guidelines calculation, sentencing factor, or noncapital enhancement; that proposition must be researched in the controlling circuit and tied to the precise bar asserted. [*McQuiggin*, 569 U.S. at 392–95](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/569bv.pdf); [*Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 623–24](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-523/pdf/USREPORTS-523-614.pdf)

## Ineffective assistance

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-035 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-MASSARO-538-US-500 status:verified_current -->
An ineffective-assistance claim may be brought first in a § 2255 motion even if the defendant could have raised it on direct appeal; failure to raise it on direct review does not itself procedurally default the claim. [*Massaro v. United States*, 538 U.S. 500, 503–09 (2003)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-538/pdf/USREPORTS-538-500.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-036 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-STRICKLAND-466-US-668 status:verified_current -->
Strickland deficiency asks whether counsel's acts or omissions fell below an objective standard of reasonableness under prevailing professional norms, judged on the facts of the particular case as of the time of counsel's conduct. The defendant must identify the challenged acts or omissions, and judicial scrutiny is highly deferential: a court must indulge a strong presumption that counsel's conduct falls within the wide range of reasonable professional assistance, which the defendant must overcome. [*Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 688–90 (1984)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-466/pdf/USREPORTS-466-668.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-037 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-STRICKLAND-466-US-668 status:verified_current -->
Ordinary Strickland prejudice requires a reasonable probability that, but for the deficient performance, the result would have been different—a probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the outcome, not proof by a preponderance that the outcome certainly would have changed. [*Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 693–96](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-466/pdf/USREPORTS-466-668.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-038 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-STRICKLAND-466-US-668 status:verified_current -->
A court may reject an ineffective-assistance claim on either Strickland component without deciding the other. [*Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 697](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-466/pdf/USREPORTS-466-668.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-039 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-STRICKLAND-466-US-668 status:verified_current -->
Judicial review of counsel is highly deferential, avoids hindsight, and presumes reasonable professional assistance, but the presumption does not replace examination of the identified conduct, record, and available alternatives. [*Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 689–91](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-466/pdf/USREPORTS-466-668.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-040 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-CRONIC-466-US-648 status:verified_current -->
*Cronic* presumes prejudice only in narrow circumstances such as complete denial of counsel at a critical stage or an entire failure to subject the prosecution's case to meaningful adversarial testing; poor performance at specific points ordinarily remains a Strickland claim. [*United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 658–67 (1984)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-466/pdf/USREPORTS-466-648.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-041 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-HILL-474-US-52 status:verified_current -->
For deficient advice leading to an accepted guilty plea, *Hill* asks whether there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's errors, the defendant would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on trial. [*Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52, 56–60 (1985)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-474/pdf/USREPORTS-474-52.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-042 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-LEE-582-US-357 status:verified_current -->
*Lee* applied *Hill v. Lockhart*'s test, a reasonable probability that but for counsel's errors the defendant would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on going to trial. It rejected a per se rule that a defendant with no viable defense cannot show prejudice, because categorical rules are ill suited to an inquiry that demands a case-by-case examination, and directed courts not to upset a plea solely because of post hoc assertions but to look to contemporaneous evidence to substantiate a defendant's expressed preferences. In the unusual circumstances of that case, where deportation was the determinative issue, the Court found a reasonable probability, backed by substantial and uncontroverted evidence, that Lee would have rejected the plea; answering the Government's argument under *Padilla*, it could not say rejecting the plea would have been irrational. [*Lee v. United States*, 582 U.S. 357, 366–71 (2017)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-582/pdf/USREPORTS-582-357.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-043 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-FRYE-566-US-134 status:verified_current -->
For a formal plea offer from the prosecution that lapsed because counsel failed to communicate it, *Frye* requires a reasonable probability that the defendant would have accepted it; that, if the prosecution had the discretion to cancel it or the trial court had the discretion to refuse it, neither would have prevented it from being accepted or implemented; and that the end result would have been more favorable by reason of a plea to a lesser charge or a sentence of less prison time. [*Missouri v. Frye*, 566 U.S. 134, 145, 147–48 (2012)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-566/pdf/USREPORTS-566-134.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-044 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-LAFLER-566-US-156 status:verified_current -->
For deficient advice causing rejection of a plea followed by a less favorable conviction or sentence, *Lafler* requires the specified probabilities concerning acceptance, nonwithdrawal, court acceptance, and a less severe result, followed by a remedy neutralizing the constitutional injury without granting a windfall. [*Lafler v. Cooper*, 566 U.S. 156, 163–75 (2012)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-566/pdf/USREPORTS-566-156.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-045 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-FLORES-ORTEGA-528-US-470 status:verified_current -->
Counsel performs deficiently by disregarding a defendant's specific instructions to file a notice of appeal, while a defendant who explicitly tells counsel not to file an appeal cannot later complain that counsel followed that instruction. Where the defendant has not clearly conveyed his wishes one way or the other, *Flores-Ortega* imposes a duty to consult about an appeal when there is reason to think either that a rational defendant would want to appeal or that this particular defendant reasonably demonstrated to counsel an interest in appealing, and prejudice requires a reasonable probability that, but for the deficient failure to consult, the defendant would have timely appealed. [*Roe v. Flores-Ortega*, 528 U.S. 470, 477–84 (2000)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-528/pdf/USREPORTS-528-470.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-046 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-GARZA-586-US-232 status:verified_current -->
When deficient failure to file a requested notice forfeits an appeal, prejudice is presumed even if the defendant signed an appeal waiver because no waiver forecloses every possible appellate claim. [*Garza v. Idaho*, 586 U.S. 232, 242–50 (2019)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-586/pdf/USREPORTS-586-232.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-047 authorities:AUTH-SCOTUS-GLOVER-531-US-198 status:verified_current -->
For an error in the sentencing calculation itself, *Glover* rejected a minimum-increase requirement for Strickland prejudice: authority does not suggest that a minimal amount of additional time in prison cannot constitute prejudice, and the Court's jurisprudence suggests that any amount of actual jail time has Sixth Amendment significance. Under a determinate system of constrained discretion such as the Sentencing Guidelines, the size of the increase cannot bar a prejudice showing, though it may be a factor in assessing deficient performance, which still must be proven. [*Glover v. United States*, 531 U.S. 198, 203–04 (2001)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-531/pdf/USREPORTS-531-198.pdf)

<!-- claim:CLM-2255-048 authorities:AUTH-USC-28-2255,AUTH-SCOTUS-GARZA-586-US-232 status:verified_current -->
Neither § 2255, which contains no waiver provision, nor *Garza* supplies a single nationwide list of enforceable or nonwaivable § 2255 claims. *Garza* addressed appeal waivers, noting that their language can vary widely, that a waiver precludes only challenges within its scope, that the prosecution can forfeit or waive the waiver, and that defendants may challenge whether the waiver itself is valid, for example as unknowing or involuntary; it made no statement on what particular exceptions may be required. [28 U.S.C. § 2255](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/28/2255); [*Garza*, 586 U.S. at 238–39 & n.6](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/586bv.pdf)

## Claim-analysis checklist

1. Identify the exact legal right, error, record event, and requested remedy.
2. Separate cognizability from timeliness, waiver, default, prior adjudication, and merits.
3. For default, identify where and how the claim could have been raised and the asserted cause.
4. For innocence, inventory all reliable evidence, including admissible government evidence and forgone charges.
5. For ineffective assistance, identify each challenged act or omission, the information then available, professional norms, and the posture-specific counterfactual.
6. Do not merge plea, appeal, trial, sentencing, and *Cronic* prejudice rules.

## Matters requiring circuit-specific work

- Relitigation of claims decided on direct appeal and exceptions for intervening law.
- Cognizability of advisory-Guidelines and other nonconstitutional sentencing errors.
- Collateral-waiver construction and recognized exceptions.
- Sentencing-innocence gateways.
- Burdens of proof, evidentiary presumptions, and remedies beyond the nationwide rules stated here.
