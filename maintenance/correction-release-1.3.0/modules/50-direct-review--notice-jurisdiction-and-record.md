#### CLM-DR-002: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> A defendant in a criminal case ordinarily must file within fourteen days after the later of entry of the judgment or order appealed from or the government's filing of a notice of appeal; the exact docket entry and any later government notice must be checked. [Fed. R. App. P. 4(b)(1)(A)]

After:

> In a criminal case, a defendant's notice of appeal must be filed in the district court within 14 days after the later of the entry of either the judgment or the order being appealed, or the filing of the government's notice of appeal. Rule 4(b)(3)(A) sets the time to appeal from a judgment of conviction when the defendant timely makes one of the motions it lists. Upon a finding of excusable neglect or good cause, the district court may extend the time to file a notice of appeal for a period not to exceed 30 days from the expiration of the time otherwise prescribed by Rule 4(b). A judgment or order is entered for purposes of Rule 4(b) when it is entered on the criminal docket. [Fed. R. App. P. 4(b)(1)(A)]

#### CLM-DR-010: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> When restitution is deferred and a later amended judgment fixes the amount, a defendant wishing to challenge that order must file a notice from it; if no notice is filed and the government timely objects, the mandatory claim-processing rule bars review of that restitution order. [*Manrique v. United States*, 581 U.S. 116, 120–25 (2017)]

After:

> When restitution is deferred and a later amended judgment imposes it, a defendant who wishes to challenge that order must file a notice of appeal from it. The Court held this requirement is at least a mandatory claim-processing rule, without deciding whether it is jurisdictional; because the defendant filed no such notice and the government timely objected, the court of appeals properly declined to review the restitution amount. [*Manrique v. United States*, 581 U.S. 116, 120–22, 125 (2017)]

Verification date: 2026-08-25 → 2026-10-30 (date of the actual review)

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review, with second and third independent checks; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Stated that the Court called the requirement 'at least' a mandatory claim-processing rule and expressly left open whether it is jurisdictional, and tied the bar to the government's timely objection as in the holding; adjusted the pinpoint to the pages relied on.
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-MANRIQUE-581-US-116, 581 U.S. at 125: "a defendant who wishes to appeal an order imposing restitution in a deferred restitution case must fle a notice of appeal from that order"
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-MANRIQUE-581-US-116, 581 U.S. at 121: "We do not need to decide in this case whether the Government is correct. The requirement that a defendant fle a timely notice of appeal from an amended judgment imposing restitution is at least a mandatory claim-processing rule."
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-MANRIQUE-581-US-116, 581 U.S. at 125: "Because petitioner failed to do so, and the Government objected, the Court of Appeals properly declined to consider his challenge to the amount of restitution imposed."

#### CLM-DR-012: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> Section 1291 gives the courts of appeals jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of the district courts except where direct Supreme Court review is authorized; identifying a criminal sentence as ordinarily final does not make every later or interlocutory order appealable. [28 U.S.C. § 1291]

After:

> Section 1291 gives the courts of appeals (other than the Federal Circuit) jurisdiction of appeals from all final decisions of the district courts, except where a direct review may be had in the Supreme Court. By its terms, § 1291 does not grant jurisdiction of appeals from a later or interlocutory order unless that order is itself a final decision. [28 U.S.C. § 1291]

Verification date: 2026-08-25 → 2026-10-31 (date of the actual review)

- **2026-10-31: Wording conformed to the text of the cited law or policy; independent reviews agreed; owner approved 2026-10-31.** Restated in the cited source's own terms (owner directive: claims follow the law and policy text). The first clause follows § 1291 ('may be had' in the statute; 'is authorized' in the claim is close). The second clause does not come from the statute. § 1291 says nothing about a criminal sentence being 'ordinarily final' or about 'later or interlocutory' orders. Its only test is 'final decisions'. The 'ordinarily final' wording should be dropped or tied to the statute's own term.
  - AUTH-USC-28-1291, 28 U.S.C. § 1291: "The courts of appeals (other than the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit) shall have jurisdiction of appeals from all final decisions of the district courts of the United States, the United States District Court for the District of the Canal Zone, the District Court of Guam, and the District Court of the Virgin Islands, except where a direct review may be had in the Supreme Court."

#### CLM-DR-014: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> A pretrial order denying a motion to dismiss on double-jeopardy grounds is immediately appealable to the extent it rejects a colorable Double Jeopardy Clause claim because the asserted right includes avoiding the second trial itself. [*Abney v. United States*, 431 U.S. 651, 659–62 (1977)]

After:

> A pretrial order denying a motion to dismiss on double-jeopardy grounds is a "final decision" that is immediately appealable to the extent it rejects the former-jeopardy claim, because the Double Jeopardy Clause is a guarantee against being twice put to trial for the same offense; other claims in the same motion are appealable only if they fall within *Cohen*'s collateral-order exception. [*Abney v. United States*, 431 U.S. 651, 660–63 (1977)]

Verification date: 2026-08-25 → 2026-10-30 (date of the actual review)

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review, with second and third independent checks; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Removed the 'colorable' threshold, which Abney does not adopt (it only notes courts of appeals may set summary procedures to weed out frivolous jeopardy claims) and no saved source supplies; stated the opinion's own limit that only the jeopardy claim is immediately appealable.
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-ABNEY-431-US-651, 431 U.S. at 662: "We therefore hold that pretrial orders rejecting claims of former jeopardy, such as that presently before us, constitute "final decisions""
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-ABNEY-431-US-651, 431 U.S. at 661: "It is a guarantee against being twice put to trial for the same offense."
