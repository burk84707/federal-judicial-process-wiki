#### CLM-2255-051: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> The government need not answer unless ordered; when ordered, Rule 5 requires it to address the allegations and specified prior-remedy matters, and the court may require relevant transcripts and briefs. [Rule 5(a)–(c), Rules Governing § 2255 Proceedings]

After:

> The government need not answer unless a judge so orders. An ordered answer must address the motion's allegations and state whether the movant has used any other federal remedies, including prior post-conviction motions, and whether the movant received an evidentiary hearing. If the answer refers to briefs or transcripts of prior proceedings that are not available in the court's records, the judge must order the government to furnish them. [Rule 5(a)–(c), Rules Governing § 2255 Proceedings]

Verification date: 2026-08-25 → 2026-10-30 (date of the actual review)

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Adopted the approved proposal: Rule 5(c) is mandatory and conditional ("must" when the answer refers to unavailable briefs or transcripts), not a discretionary "may require."
  - AUTH-RG-2255-4-12, Rule 5(a): "The respondent is not required to answer the motion unless a judge so orders."
  - AUTH-RG-2255-4-12, Rule 5(b): "The answer must address the allegations in the motion."
  - AUTH-RG-2255-4-12, Rule 5(c): "the judge must order the government to furnish them within a reasonable time that will not unduly delay the proceedings"

#### CLM-2255-057: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> Plea-hearing declarations carry a strong presumption of verity, but they are not invariably insurmountable; a sufficiently specific collateral allegation and the full record determine whether summary dismissal or further proceedings are proper. [*Blackledge v. Allison*, 431 U.S. 63, 73–82 (1977)]

After:

> Plea-hearing declarations carry a strong presumption of verity, and conclusory allegations unsupported by specifics are subject to summary dismissal, but the plea-record barrier is not invariably insurmountable. [*Blackledge v. Allison*, 431 U.S. 63, 74, 80–82 & n.19 (1977)]

#### CLM-2255-058: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> If an evidentiary hearing is warranted, Rule 8(c) requires appointment of counsel for a financially eligible movant; outside mandatory rule-based circumstances, counsel is not automatically guaranteed by this rule. [Rule 8(c), Rules Governing § 2255 Proceedings]

After:

> If an evidentiary hearing is warranted, Rule 8(c) requires the judge to appoint an attorney to represent a moving party who qualifies to have counsel appointed under 18 U.S.C. § 3006A. Rule 6(a) likewise requires the judge to appoint an attorney for such a moving party if necessary for effective discovery. Outside those two rules, the § 2255 Rules do not require appointment of counsel, but these rules do not limit the appointment of counsel under § 3006A at any stage of the proceeding. [Rule 8(c), Rules Governing § 2255 Proceedings]

  - AUTH-RG-2255-4-12, Rule 8(c): "These rules do not limit the appointment of counsel under § 3006A at any stage of the proceeding."
- **2026-10-31: Wording conformed to the text of the cited law or policy; independent reviews agreed; owner approved 2026-10-31.** Restated in the cited source's own terms (owner directive: claims follow the law and policy text). #### CLM-2255-060: rewritten

#### CLM-2255-064: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> The certificate inquiry is a threshold debatability assessment, not full merits adjudication; a court may not first decide the merits against the applicant and then deny a certificate because the applicant lost that merits determination. [*Miller-El v. Cockrell*, 537 U.S. 322, 336–42 (2003)]; [*Buck v. Davis*, 580 U.S. 100, 115–23 (2017)]

After:

> In the court of appeals, the certificate inquiry is a threshold debatability assessment, not full merits adjudication; a court of appeals that first decides the merits of an appeal and then denies a certificate based on that adjudication is in essence deciding an appeal without jurisdiction. [*Miller-El v. Cockrell*, 537 U.S. 322, 336–37 (2003)]; [*Buck v. Davis*, 580 U.S. 100, 115–17 (2017)] The district court, by contrast, must issue or deny a certificate when it enters a final order adverse to the applicant. [Rule 11(a), Rules Governing § 2255 Proceedings]

Authorities: AUTH-SCOTUS-MILLER-EL-537-US-322, AUTH-SCOTUS-BUCK-580-US-100 → AUTH-SCOTUS-MILLER-EL-537-US-322, AUTH-SCOTUS-BUCK-580-US-100, AUTH-RG-2255-4-12

Verification date: 2026-08-25 → 2026-10-30 (date of the actual review)

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Confined the no-merits-first rule to the court of appeals, as both opinions state it, added Rule 11(a)'s district-court duty to issue or deny a certificate after its merits ruling, and tightened the pinpoints.

  - AUTH-SCOTUS-BUCK-580-US-100, 580 U.S. at 115: "The COA inquiry, we have emphasized, is not coextensive with a merits analysis."
