#### CLM-CIR3-003: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> In the Third Circuit, § 404 does not itself entitle a defendant to an in-person or plenary resentencing hearing, but the defendant must receive a reasonable opportunity to present written sentencing arguments, and the court must give enough explanation for meaningful appellate review. [*United States v. Shields*, 48 F.4th 183, 195–202 (3d Cir. 2022)]

After:

> In the Third Circuit, a First Step Act § 404 movant ordinarily is not entitled to a plenary resentencing hearing at which he would be present, and neither the original sentencing judge nor a reassigned judge is required to hold an in-person hearing; the court must still articulate a brief statement of reasons demonstrating that it considered the parties’ arguments. Where the movant requested a hearing or, alternatively, leave to file a sentencing memorandum, the district court erred by denying him “either a hearing or a reasonable opportunity to present his sentencing arguments in writing.” The court of appeals commended, but declined to mandate, a specific multistep procedure that several district courts in the circuit had adopted, which includes giving the parties an opportunity to file sentencing memoranda. [*United States v. Shields*, 48 F.4th 183, slip op. at 14–20 (3d Cir. 2022)]

#### CLM-CIR3-005: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> In the Third Circuit, § 3583(e)(1) does not require an exceptional, extraordinary, new, or unforeseen circumstance before early termination may be granted, although changed circumstances may often be relevant to the discretionary assessment. [*United States v. Melvin*, 978 F.3d 49, 52–53 (3d Cir. 2020)]

After:

> In the Third Circuit, a district court need not find an exceptional, extraordinary, new, or unforeseen circumstance before granting early termination under § 3583(e)(1), although Melvin states that early termination generally will be proper only when the judge is satisfied that new or unforeseen circumstances warrant it. [*United States v. Melvin*, 978 F.3d 49, slip op. at 6–7 (3d Cir. 2020)] The court may terminate supervision only after the expiration of one year of supervised release and only if satisfied that termination is warranted by the defendant’s conduct and the interest of justice. [18 U.S.C. § 3583(e)(1)]
