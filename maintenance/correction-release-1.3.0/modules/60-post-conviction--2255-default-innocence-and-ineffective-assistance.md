#### CLM-2255-023: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> Section 2255 reaches federal constitutional and statutory violations, lack of sentencing jurisdiction, a sentence above the legal maximum, and other grounds satisfying the statute's collateral-attack standard; the label attached to a claim does not establish cognizability. [28 U.S.C. § 2255(a)–(b)]

After:

> Section 2255 reaches sentences imposed in violation of the Constitution or laws of the United States, sentences imposed without jurisdiction, sentences in excess of the maximum authorized by law, and sentences otherwise subject to collateral attack. [28 U.S.C. § 2255(a)–(b)] The remedy does not encompass all claimed errors: outside jurisdictional and constitutional claims, an error of law does not provide a basis for collateral attack unless it constituted "a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice." [*United States v. Addonizio*, 442 U.S. 178, 185 (1979)]; [*Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962)]

Authorities: AUTH-USC-28-2255 → AUTH-USC-28-2255, AUTH-SCOTUS-ADDONIZIO-442-US-178, AUTH-SCOTUS-HILL-368-US-424

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Used the statute's word "laws" instead of "statutory," replaced the unsupported label sentence with Addonizio's statement that the remedy does not reach all errors, and added the fundamental-defect limit on nonconstitutional claims (Davis is not saved, so Addonizio and Hill are cited).

#### CLM-2255-024: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> A nonconstitutional violation of a criminal rule is not automatically cognizable: *Hill* required a jurisdictional or constitutional error, a fundamental defect inherently resulting in a complete miscarriage of justice, or an omission inconsistent with rudimentary demands of fair procedure. [*Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428–30 (1962)]

After:

> A nonconstitutional violation of a criminal rule is not automatically cognizable: in *Hill*, the failure to invite the defendant to speak before sentence was neither jurisdictional nor constitutional, was not a fundamental defect inherently resulting in a complete miscarriage of justice or an omission inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure, and did not present exceptional circumstances where the need for the remedy is apparent. The Court decided only that § 2255 relief is unavailable when all that is shown is a failure to comply with the formal requirements of Rule 32(a), leaving open violations occurring with other aggravating circumstances. [*Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428–29 (1962)]

  - AUTH-SCOTUS-HILL-368-US-424, 368 U.S. at 428: "It is an error which is neither jurisdictional nor constitutional."
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-HILL-368-US-424, 368 U.S. at 428: "exceptional circumstances where the need for the remedy afforded by the writ of habeas corpus is apparent"

#### CLM-2255-026: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> A sentencing judge's disappointed expectation about later executive administration of a lawful sentence was not a § 2255 defect in *Addonizio*; collateral relief requires an error of the fundamental character recognized by the statute and cases, not merely a later outcome different from an unexpressed expectation. [*United States v. Addonizio*, 442 U.S. 178, 184–90 (1979)]

After:

> A sentencing judge's disappointed expectation about later Parole Commission administration of a lawful sentence was not a § 2255 defect in *Addonizio*: the judge has no enforceable expectations about actual release, and later Commission actions, whether or not they accord with the judge's expectations at sentencing, do not retroactively affect the validity of the final judgment. Absent a claim of constitutional error or lack of jurisdiction, or a sentence outside statutory limits, collateral relief requires an error of fact or law of the "fundamental" character that renders the entire proceeding irregular and invalid. [*United States v. Addonizio*, 442 U.S. 178, 185–90 (1979)]

Verification date: 2026-08-25 → 2026-10-30 (date of the actual review)

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Removed the "unexpressed expectation" qualifier, which implied an expressed expectation might suffice, and stated Addonizio's holding that the judge has no enforceable expectations whether or not later action matches them.
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-ADDONIZIO-442-US-178, 442 U.S. at 186: "the proceeding was not infected with any error of fact or law of the “fundamental” character that renders the entire proceeding irregular and invalid"
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-ADDONIZIO-442-US-178, 442 U.S. at 190: "the judge has no enforceable expectations with respect to the actual release of a sentenced defendant short of his statutory term"
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-ADDONIZIO-442-US-178, 442 U.S. at 190: "do not retroactively affect the validity of the final judgment itself"

#### CLM-2255-031: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> When a plea bargain caused the government to forgo more serious charges, *Bousley* permits the government to present any admissible evidence of guilt and requires the movant's innocence showing to extend to those forgone charges. [*Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 624]

After:

> On a *Bousley* actual-innocence showing, the government is not limited to the existing record and may present any admissible evidence of guilt, even evidence not presented at the plea colloquy; separately, where the government has forgone more serious charges in the course of plea bargaining, the movant's innocence showing must also extend to those charges. *Bousley* did not extend the showing to a charge absent record evidence that the government elected not to bring it in exchange for the plea. [*Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 624]

#### CLM-2255-036: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> Strickland deficiency asks whether counsel's acts or omissions fell below an objective standard of reasonableness under prevailing professional norms and the circumstances as they existed then. [*Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687–91 (1984)]

> Strickland deficiency asks whether counsel's acts or omissions fell below an objective standard of reasonableness under prevailing professional norms, judged on the facts of the particular case as of the time of counsel's conduct. #### CLM-2255-042: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> *Lee* requires a case-specific assessment of whether rejecting the plea would have been rational and relies on contemporaneous evidence rather than post hoc assertions; strong evidence of guilt does not categorically foreclose plea prejudice where the consequence driving the decision was deportation. [*Lee v. United States*, 582 U.S. 357, 364–71 (2017)]

After:

> *Lee* applied *Hill v. Lockhart*'s test, a reasonable probability that but for counsel's errors the defendant would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on going to trial. It rejected a per se rule that a defendant with no viable defense cannot show prejudice, because categorical rules are ill suited to an inquiry that demands a case-by-case examination, and directed courts not to upset a plea solely because of post hoc assertions but to look to contemporaneous evidence to substantiate a defendant's expressed preferences. In the unusual circumstances of that case, where deportation was the determinative issue, the Court found a reasonable probability, backed by substantial and uncontroverted evidence, that Lee would have rejected the plea; answering the Government's argument under *Padilla*, it could not say rejecting the plea would have been irrational. [*Lee v. United States*, 582 U.S. 357, 366–71 (2017)]

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Stated that the governing prejudice test remains Hill v. Lockhart's, added Lee's "solely" qualifier and its unusual-circumstances limit, and narrowed the pinpoint to 367–71.

#### CLM-2255-043: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> For a favorable plea offer that lapsed because counsel failed to communicate it, *Frye* requires a reasonable probability the defendant would have accepted it, the prosecution would not have withdrawn it, the court would have accepted it, and the result would have been less severe. #### CLM-2255-045: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> Counsel performs deficiently by disregarding a defendant's express instruction to file an appeal; absent a clear instruction, *Flores-Ortega* applies a circumstance-specific duty to consult and requires prejudice tied to a reasonable probability the defendant would have appealed. [*Roe v. Flores-Ortega*, 528 U.S. 470, 477–86 (2000)]

After:

> Counsel performs deficiently by disregarding a defendant's specific instructions to file a notice of appeal, while a defendant who explicitly tells counsel not to file an appeal cannot later complain that counsel followed that instruction. Where the defendant has not clearly conveyed his wishes one way or the other, *Flores-Ortega* imposes a duty to consult about an appeal when there is reason to think either that a rational defendant would want to appeal or that this particular defendant reasonably demonstrated to counsel an interest in appealing, and prejudice requires a reasonable probability that, but for the deficient failure to consult, the defendant would have timely appealed. [*Roe v. Flores-Ortega*, 528 U.S. 470, 477–84 (2000)]

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Replaced "circumstance-specific duty" with the two triggers that create the duty to consult, restored "timely" in the prejudice test, and set the pinpoint to 477–84.

#### CLM-2255-047: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> There is no minimum amount of additional imprisonment required for Strickland prejudice from a sentencing error; any increased prison time has Sixth Amendment significance, although deficiency and causation still must be proven. [*Glover v. United States*, 531 U.S. 198, 202–04 (2001)]

After:

> For an error in the sentencing calculation itself, *Glover* rejected a minimum-increase requirement for Strickland prejudice: authority does not suggest that a minimal amount of additional time in prison cannot constitute prejudice, and the Court's jurisprudence suggests that any amount of actual jail time has Sixth Amendment significance. Under a determinate system of constrained discretion such as the Sentencing Guidelines, the size of the increase cannot bar a prejudice showing, though it may be a factor in assessing deficient performance, which still must be proven. [*Glover v. United States*, 531 U.S. 198, 203–04 (2001)]

Verification date: 2026-08-25 → 2026-10-30 (date of the actual review)

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Restored Glover's "suggests" wording instead of a flat rule, tied the holding to sentencing-calculation errors under a constrained-discretion system like the Guidelines, noted the size of the increase may bear on deficiency, and narrowed the pinpoint to 203–04.
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-GLOVER-531-US-198, 531 U.S. at 203: "our jurisprudence suggests that any amount of actual jail time has Sixth Amendment significance"

#### CLM-2255-048: rewritten

[28 U.S.C. § 2255]; [*Garza*, 586 U.S. at 238–50]

[28 U.S.C. § 2255]; [*Garza*, 586 U.S. at 238–39 & n.6]
