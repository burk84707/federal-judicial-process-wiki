#### CLM-MOTION-010: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> A *Franks* evidentiary hearing requires a substantial preliminary showing that a warrant-affidavit false statement was made knowingly and intentionally or with reckless disregard for truth and was necessary to probable cause; conclusory allegations or a mere desire to cross-examine do not suffice. [*Franks v. Delaware*, 438 U.S. 154, 155–56, 170–72 (1978)]

After:

> A *Franks* evidentiary hearing requires a substantial preliminary showing that the affiant included a false statement in the warrant affidavit knowingly and intentionally or with reckless disregard for the truth, and that it was necessary to the finding of probable cause; the attack must be more than conclusory and more than a mere desire to cross-examine, the allegations must be accompanied by an offer of proof, should point out specifically the portion claimed false with supporting reasons, and should be supported by affidavits or sworn or otherwise reliable witness statements or a satisfactory explanation of their absence. Allegations of negligence or innocent mistake are insufficient, and the deliberate falsity or reckless disregard at issue is only that of the affiant, not of any nongovernmental informant. [*Franks v. Delaware*, 438 U.S. 154, 155–56, 171–72 (1978)]

Verification date: 2026-08-25 → 2026-10-30 (date of the actual review)

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review, with second and third independent checks; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Added Franks's offer-of-proof and specificity requirements, the insufficiency of negligence or innocent mistake, and the limit to the affiant's own falsity or recklessness.
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-FRANKS-438-US-154, 438 U.S. at 155-56 (PDF pp. 2-3): "a false statement knowingly and intentionally, or with reckless disregard for the truth, was included by the affiant in the warrant affidavit, and if the allegedly false statement is necessary to the finding of probable cause"

#### CLM-MOTION-015: rewritten

After:

> *Miranda* holds that the prosecution may not use statements, whether exculpatory or inculpatory, stemming from custodial interrogation unless it demonstrates the use of procedural safeguards: unless other fully effective means are devised, the specified warnings are required, and a heavy burden rests on the government to demonstrate a knowing and intelligent waiver. Later decisions have reduced the rule's impact while reaffirming its core ruling that unwarned statements may not be used as evidence in the prosecution's case in chief. [*Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436, 444–45, 475, 479 (1966)]; [*Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443–44 (2000)]

#### CLM-MOTION-024: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> *Zafiro* directs relief only when a joint trial creates a serious risk of compromising a specific trial right or preventing a reliable guilt-or-innocence judgment, and even then the remedy need not be severance because limiting instructions or other measures may suffice. [*Zafiro*, 506 U.S. at 539–41]

After:

> Under *Zafiro*, when defendants properly have been joined under Rule 8(b), a district court should grant a severance under Rule 14 only if there is a serious risk that a joint trial would compromise a specific trial right of one of the defendants or prevent the jury from making a reliable judgment about guilt or innocence. Even if prejudice is shown, Rule 14 does not require severance; it leaves the tailoring of relief, if any, to the district court's sound discretion, and less drastic measures, such as limiting instructions, often will suffice. [*Zafiro v. United States*, 506 U.S. 534, 538–39, 541 (1993)]

#### CLM-MOTION-030: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> On appeal after a suppression ruling, *Ornelas* requires independent review of ultimate reasonable-suspicion and probable-cause determinations, while historical facts receive clear-error review and due weight is given to inferences drawn by resident judges and local officers. After:

> On appeal after a ruling on reasonable suspicion to stop or probable cause for a warrantless search, *Ornelas* holds that as a general matter those ultimate determinations are reviewed de novo, while findings of historical fact are reviewed only for clear error and due weight is given to inferences drawn by resident judges and local law enforcement officers; the Court contrasted the lesser scrutiny applied to a magistrate's probable-cause determination to issue a warrant. [*Ornelas v. United States*, 517 U.S. 690, 698–700 (1996)]
