#### CLM-2255-004: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> Section 2255(e) ordinarily makes the sentencing-court remedy exclusive for a federal prisoner attacking detention under the sentence, subject to the saving-clause boundary addressed separately; inability to satisfy § 2255's gatekeeping provisions does not by itself establish that the remedy is inadequate or ineffective. [28 U.S.C. § 2255(e)]

After:

> Section 2255(e) ordinarily makes the sentencing-court remedy exclusive for a federal prisoner attacking detention under the sentence, subject to the saving-clause boundary addressed separately. [28 U.S.C. § 2255(e)] A prisoner with a statutory claim who cannot satisfy § 2255(h)'s two conditions for a second or successive motion cannot bring that claim in a habeas petition under the saving clause. [*Jones v. Hendrix*, 599 U.S. 465, 480 (2023)]

Authorities: AUTH-USC-28-2255 → AUTH-USC-28-2255, AUTH-SCOTUS-JONES-HENDRIX-599-US-465

Verification date: 2026-08-25 → 2026-10-30 (date of the actual review)

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Attributed the gatekeeping point to Jones v. Hendrix (not § 2255(e)'s text) and narrowed it to Jones's actual holding about statutory claims that fail § 2255(h).
  - AUTH-USC-28-2255, § 2255(e): "shall not be entertained if it appears that the applicant has failed to apply for relief, by motion, to the court which sentenced him"
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-JONES-HENDRIX-599-US-465, 599 U.S. at 480: "The inability of a prisoner with a statutory claim to satisfy those conditions does not mean that he can bring his claim in a habeas petition under the saving clause."

#### CLM-2255-010: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> Section 2255(f) supplies one year from the latest applicable date among four separately worded triggers; a movant must identify and prove the trigger actually relied on rather than treating all four as extensions of finality. [28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)]

After:

> Section 2255(f) supplies a one-year limitation period that runs from the latest of four separately worded dates: the date on which the judgment of conviction becomes final, or one of three other dates that apply only on their own terms: the date on which an impediment to making a motion created by governmental action in violation of the Constitution or laws of the United States is removed, if the movant was prevented from making a motion by that action; the date on which the right asserted was initially recognized by the Supreme Court, if that right has been newly recognized by the Supreme Court and made retroactively applicable to cases on collateral review; or the date on which the facts supporting the claim could have been discovered through the exercise of due diligence. [28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)]

#### CLM-2255-022: rewritten

Before (public 1.2.1):

> *Day* permits a habeas court to consider a nonjurisdictional time bar not correctly raised by the State only after fair notice and an opportunity to respond and without overriding a deliberate waiver; because *Day* concerned § 2254 and § 2244(d), a § 2255 court must also apply controlling-circuit law and the government's actual position. [*Day v. McDonough*, 547 U.S. 198, 205–11 (2006)]

After:

> In the circumstances presented there, an evident miscalculation of the elapsed time, *Day* held that district courts are permitted, but not obliged, to consider sua sponte the timeliness of a state prisoner's habeas petition, under a limitation defense that is not jurisdictional, even though the State did not correctly raise it. Before acting on its own initiative, the court must accord the parties fair notice and an opportunity to present their positions, must assure itself that the petitioner is not significantly prejudiced by the delayed focus on the limitation issue, and must determine whether the interests of justice would be better served by addressing the merits or by dismissing the petition as time barred; it would be an abuse of discretion to override a State's deliberate waiver of a limitations defense. *Day* addressed a state prisoner's petition under § 2244(d)'s one-year limitation. [*Day v. McDonough*, 547 U.S. 198, 202 & n.1, 205, 209–10 & n.11 (2006)]

Verification date: 2026-08-25 → 2026-10-30 (date of the actual review)

- **2026-10-30: Dual independent source review; owner approved 2026-10-30.** Added Day's prejudice and interests-of-justice conditions, its permitted-not-obliged framing and its circumstances-presented limit, and corrected the pinpoint to 202, 209–10.
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-DAY-547-US-198, 547 U.S. at 202: "In the circumstances here presented, we hold, the federal court had discretion to correct the State's error"
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-DAY-547-US-198, 547 U.S. at 202: "we would count it an abuse of discretion to override a State's deliberate waiver"
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-DAY-547-US-198, 547 U.S. at 209: "In sum, we hold that district courts are permitted, but"
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-DAY-547-US-198, 547 U.S. at 210: "a court must accord the parties fair notice and an opportunity to present their positions"
  - AUTH-SCOTUS-DAY-547-US-198, 547 U.S. at 210: "the court must assure itself that the petitioner is not significantly prejudiced by the delayed focus on the limitation issue"
